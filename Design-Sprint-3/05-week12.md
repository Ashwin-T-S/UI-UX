## 2. Why We Are Doing It

A working product designer in India is asked, sooner or later, to ship something that misleads a user: a fake timer, a guilt trip on a cancel button, a consent box that is already ticked. Saying "that is wrong" rarely works. Saying "here is the risk, here is the alternative, here is what I will still deliver" usually does. That is a job skill, and it is also the thing senior interviewers test with a scenario question.

This is also the week the subscription flow becomes portfolio material. It is a consumer product with real visual range, so three hero screens from it go on the front of your portfolio. The craft of those three screens matters more than the number of screens you made.

## 3. What to Learn First

By the end of the week you can:

- State an ethics position that is specific enough to be tested
- Write a refusal that a manager could actually receive without dismissing you
- Ship an accessible, fully specified interaction design
- Defend a line under pressure without either collapsing or making dramatic claims

Core ideas to review first:

- A position you cannot point at on a specific screen, in a specific meeting, is decoration.
- Most design codes of ethics are vague enough to be useless in a meeting. Codes written for computing are more specific, because computing has had to defend itself in court.
- A refusal is not a manifesto. Short, specific, with an alternative and a cost estimate, gets read.
- A status change that a sighted user sees as a banner is silent for a screen reader user unless you specify a live region. A live region is a part of the page that announces changes out loud. You specify it in the design this week. You test it in the browser in week12b.
- WCAG 2.2.1 (timing): if you keep any timed element, it must be adjustable, extendable or turn-off-able.
- Figma file rule: ONE Figma file per sprint, named `SPRINT-3 UX{n}`, with one page per week. Not three files per sprint. The free Starter plan allows 3 files in total. Full detail: `../04-Tools-Setup.md`.

### Group 1 - Your line

#### Task - Your ethics position (ID `E2.1`)

##### 1. What We Are Doing

Write a five-section statement of where your ethical line is.

##### 2. Why We Are Doing It

You will need it in the defense on Day 5, and in a real job you will need it in a room where nobody has written one. A position with named practices can be tested. A position made of good intentions cannot.

##### 3. What to Learn First

Read the framework in `06-ethics-position.md`. Notice that most design codes of ethics are vague enough to be useless in a meeting, while the ACM's is more specific because computing has had to defend itself in court. Yours should be closer to the ACM's: usable, not aspirational.

##### 4. Where to Find Resources

- `06-ethics-position.md` (20 min)
- AIGA or IxDA code of conduct (20 min), search for it, check before use
- ACM Code of Ethics, sections 1 and 2 (35 min)

##### 5. Step-by-Step Guide

1. Open a new markdown file and write the heading "1. What I will not do".
2. List specific practices, named. Not "I will not deceive users". Name the patterns.
3. Write section 2, "What I will do under protest", and define what "under protest" means concretely. For example: I build it, and I put the objection in writing in the decision record.
4. Write section 3, "What I consider acceptable persuasion", using your test from E1.2.
5. Write section 4, "How I would raise a concern". Start with one line: who you tell, and in what order.
6. Add to section 4 one line: in writing or verbally, and what you do if nothing changes.
7. Write section 5, "What I would need in order to refuse". List the things: savings, seniority, a written policy, a union, another offer.
8. Mark beside each item in section 5 whether you have it today or not. This is where most positions become believable, or stop being believable.
9. Check that every item in section 1 is something you could point at on a screen.

**Practice example, section 1 (a different product, to show the shape):**

> **What I will not do:**
> - I will not show a permission prompt before the user has done anything that needs that permission.
> - I will not write a "Skip" label that hides what is being skipped.
> - I will not make the "Later" option a small grey text link while the "Allow" option is a full-width button.
>
> Notice three things: each line names a pattern, not a feeling. Each line is visible on one screen. Each line is short enough to quote in a meeting.

This is a practice example from a different product. Do not copy it. Your own section 1 must come from the products you audited in E1.3.

**How to think about it:** start from screens you have actually seen, not from values. If a sentence cannot be checked against a frame, rewrite it until it can.

If you get stuck: reread the section of `06-ethics-position.md` that covers refusals. Search "design ethics position statement example". Ask in Discord with a screenshot of your section 1 and say which items you are unsure about.

#### Task - The written refusal (ID `E2.2`)

##### 1. What We Are Doing

Write the message you would send a manager who asked you to ship the confirmshaming and the fake timer from E1.3.

##### 2. Why We Are Doing It

This is the moment the ethics position meets a real inbox. A manager does not file a good refusal under "difficult". They act on it. That is the skill.

##### 3. What to Learn First

Finish **Ruined by Design**. Then re-read your own E1.4 point 5, the sentences you wrote for the manager conversation, and ask whether you would actually say them out loud. A refusal that opens with what you are delivering, isolates one or two items, gives external authority, offers an alternative with an honest note on cost, and ends with a commitment to work, gets read.

##### 4. Where to Find Resources

- Ruined by Design, the remainder (60 min)
- Your own E1.3 and E1.4 work from Sprint 2

##### 5. Step-by-Step Guide

1. Set the scenario in one line: your manager asked for the confirmshaming label and the fake timer.
2. Open with what you will deliver, so the message is not read as a refusal to work.
3. State what you will not do. One or two things, not a list of grievances.
4. Give the reason in terms your manager cares about: regulatory exposure, chargebacks, support load, reputational risk. Not "it is wrong".
5. Offer the alternative you will build, immediately, with an honest note on what changes relative to what was asked: effort, risk, and any effect you cannot predict.
6. Name what you will do instead of nothing. You are refusing one specific thing, not the job.
7. Say what you would do if the decision goes against you: state whether you would proceed under protest, escalate, or refuse, and explain how that choice follows your E2.1 position.
8. Cut it to under 300 words.

**Worked example (a different scenario, to show the structure):**

> Subject: Contacts upload - one change I need to flag before I build
>
> I can have the onboarding screens and the dashboard ready by Thursday. One item in the brief needs a short conversation first.
>
> **Requiring a contacts upload before the dashboard opens.** As specified, a new user cannot reach the dashboard until they upload their contacts. That is a gate, not a choice. App store review guidance and data protection rules both look closely at consent that is not freely given. I will build the same screen as a skippable step, with a clear reason for the upload and a "Not now" option that keeps full access. The effect on sign-ups is unknown, so I propose we test both versions with a small group rather than estimate it now.
>
> Everything else in the brief I will build as written.
>
> If you still want the gate after reading this, tell me. I will answer in writing before I start: I proceed under protest with my objection in the decision log, or I escalate, or I refuse. Which one it is follows from my own position, not from how the meeting goes.
>
> Ready to start today either way.

Note the structure: it opens with what is being delivered, it isolates one specific item rather than resisting the whole brief, it gives external authority rather than personal preference, it offers a concrete alternative and says what cannot be predicted, it does not threaten, and it ends with a commitment to work.

**How to think about it:** write it for a busy reader who will forward it. Every sentence should either reduce their risk or reduce their work.

If you get stuck: reread your E1.3 dark pattern audit and pick the two items you feel strongest about. Search "how to push back on a design decision at work". Ask in Discord with your draft and the word count.

#### Task - Accessibility pass (ID `C12.2`)

##### 1. What We Are Doing

Review the shipped flow on the design side, and write down the accessibility behaviour you are specifying: focus order, focus return, and how each status change is announced. You specify it this week. You run the browser keyboard test and the screen reader test in week12b, when the screen is real HTML.

##### 2. Why We Are Doing It

Your flow is full of status changes: payment processing, plan changed, cancellation scheduled. A sighted user sees a banner appear. A screen reader user hears nothing unless the design names a live region. That decision is made in the design. A coded page cannot announce something the design never named, so the specification is the part you own this week.

##### 3. What to Learn First

Read the ARIA Authoring Practices Guide Dialog (Modal) and Alert patterns, including the keyboard tables. Read WCAG 4.1.3 Status Messages, and 2.4.7 again. You are reading these to write a specification, not to test code.

##### 4. Where to Find Resources

- ARIA Authoring Practices Guide, Dialog (Modal) and Alert patterns (35 min), search for it, check before use
- WCAG, https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/quickref/, 4.1.3 and 2.4.7 (25 min)
- VoiceOver on Mac (Cmd + F5) or NVDA on Windows (https://www.nvaccess.org/). Read the setup now, in `../04-Tools-Setup.md`. You run the test in week12b.
- Figma, in the same `SPRINT-3 UX{n}` file. Setup is in `../04-Tools-Setup.md`.

##### 5. Step-by-Step Guide

1. List every state change in the flow that a user needs to be told about.
2. Beside each one, write where it appears: banner, inline message, or modal.
3. Write the live region for each: polite or assertive, and why.
4. Write the focus order for one screen, stop by stop.
5. Repeat for every screen in the flow.
6. For each modal, specify four behaviours: focus moves in on open, focus is trapped while open, Escape closes, focus returns to the trigger on close.
7. Write the visible focus style: colour, thickness, offset, and where it must be visible.
8. Record the contrast values for text, disabled states and focus rings. Write the numbers, not "looks fine".
9. Write the reduced-motion version beside every animation you specified.
10. If you kept any timed element, apply WCAG 2.2.1: it must be adjustable, extendable or turn-off-able.
11. If your ethics position already removed the only timer, say so. It is worth pointing out in the defense.
12. End the file with one line: "Browser keyboard test and screen reader test: week12b, task Y1.5."

If you get stuck: read your list of state changes out loud, one by one, and write what the user should hear for each. Search "WCAG 4.1.3 status messages aria-live polite assertive". Ask in Discord with the screen where you cannot decide what to announce.

#### Task - Defense (no task ID)

##### 1. What We Are Doing

A 60 minute live defense of your interaction design and your ethics position.

##### 2. Why We Are Doing It

##### 3. What to Learn First

##### 4. Where to Find Resources

- Two or three peers from Discord, booked for the slot
- The constraint cards below

##### 5. Step-by-Step Guide

1. Two days before, post the slot in Discord and book two or three peers for 60 minutes.
2. Agree the roles in the same message: one facilitator keeps time and reads the prompts, one feedback role writes notes.
3. Send the facilitator the parts table below before the call.
4. Run the session.

| Part | Time | What you do |
|---|---|---|
| Interaction walk | 10 min | The state machine and three transitions, from memory |
| Motion defense | 10 min | Three animations: duration, easing, purpose, and reduced-motion variant |
| Dark pattern | 10 min | What you built, its mechanism, and its cost |
| Ethics position | 10 min | Where your line is and why it is there |
| The pressure | 10 min | You are pushed to ship the dark version. Maintain or change your decision, and justify it. |
| Alternative | 10 min | A constraint card is drawn |

5. After the call, ask the feedback role to send you their notes.
6. Copy the notes into `P12-1-reflection.md`.
7. Write down the question you answered worst.

**On the pressure section.** Both failure modes are weak answers. Collapsing immediately, "okay, I'd ship it" with no attempt to negotiate, shows you have no position. Making dramatic claims, "I would quit on the spot", is not credible from a junior designer with no savings, and the group will say so. The pass is a real negotiation: what you concede, what you hold, what you escalate, and what you put in writing.

**Sprint 3 constraint cards:** all animation is banned by the platform team · the business requires the dark version and you have no power to influence the decision · a WCAG audit lands mid-sprint and blocks release · the payment provider's API forces a 6-second wait on cancellation · legal requires a 3-screen consent flow you did not want.

If you get stuck: rehearse the interaction walk once out loud the day before. If you cannot answer a question, say what you would check and who you would ask, instead of guessing.

##### 5. Step-by-Step Guide

1. Choose three hero screens from the shipped flow, for example the plan chooser, a lesson-complete moment, and the pause confirmation.
2. Write your type pairing decision: which fonts, which sizes for heading, body and caption. Result: a type scale you can point at on all three screens.
3. Write your imagery or illustration approach, and the rights you have to use it. Result: one line per image.
4. Set one colour decision per screen and write the reason beside it. Result: three notes, one per screen.
5. Set depth and spacing: choose one elevation and one spacing scale, and apply both to all three screens. Result: the same spacing scale on all three.
6. Polish one signature transition to the C11.1 motion spec. Result: duration, easing and purpose written down, plus the reduced-motion version.
7. Export the three screens at 2x. Name the files lowercase with hyphens.
8. Record a 20-second prototype clip, upload it outside the repo, and keep a view-only link.
9. Write a 100-word caption that states the problem, the decision, and the result you can claim.
10. Check the three screens still pass contrast and still have a reduced-motion version.

**How to think about it:** three screens done well beat ten done evenly. Every claim in the caption must be something you can show.
