About 21 hours of work in this week.

## 1. What We Are Doing

This week you design a dark pattern on purpose, then document the harm it causes and build an honest version. A dark pattern is a design choice that tricks or pressures users. You will work with a real cancellation flow, not a toy example.

Task groups:
- Group 1: E1.1 dark pattern audit.
- Group 2: E1.2 persuasion versus manipulation test.
- Group 3: E1.3 build the dark pattern.
- Group 4: E1.4 cost accounting, E1.5 honest version, critique, whiteboard why.
- Group 5: X3 post mortem, P11.1 principle conflict.
- Group 6: S13.1 to S13.3 system rules, F11.1 to F11.3 fundamentals.

This week is uncomfortable on purpose. You cannot reliably recognise manipulation in your own work if you have only studied other people's examples. Everything you build this week stays in the repository, clearly labelled. Nothing goes on your portfolio without its documentation attached.

## 2. Why We Are Doing It

A working product designer in India will meet retention targets, growth requests and subscription flows. Teams often ask for small pressure tactics one at a time. Each one can look harmless. You need to name the mechanism, name the legal exposure and offer a business argument for the honest version. That is a real workplace skill.

For the project, this week tests whether your principles can survive a business demand. For the portfolio, this week gives you a strong case study only if you show all three parts: the dark version, the cost accounting and the honest version. Do not show the dark version alone.

## 3. What to Learn First

By the end of the week you can:
- Name the mechanism behind a dark pattern, not just point at it.
- Tell the difference between persuasion and manipulation with a stated test.
- Build a manipulative flow and account for exactly what it takes from the user.
- Respond to a business request that conflicts with your own principles.

Core ideas to review first:
- A label is weak. A mechanism tells you how the trick works and how to detect it elsewhere.
- Persuasion shows a clear reason and still lets the user choose freely. Manipulation works because of a limit in attention, memory or emotion.
- Learn the taxonomy names: confirmshaming, roach motel, privacy zuckering, forced continuity, sneak into basket, misdirection, obstruction, nagging, hidden costs.
- Fogg Behaviour Model: motivation, ability, prompt.
- Nir Eyal's Hooked loop: trigger, action, reward, investment. Read it critically, not as a rule book.
- CMA and FTC reports describe practices that have produced fines and consent decrees. "The business asked for it" is not a defence that has worked.
- Retention lift means the estimated increase in the number of customers who stay.
- Point 5 in cost accounting is not a moral speech. Name the exposure, offer an alternative, concede the commercial cost and ask who owns the risk.
- The honest version needs a business argument. "It is the right thing to do" loses the meeting.
- Minimum detectable effect: the smallest change your test can see. Guardrail: a second metric that must not get worse.
- Figma free plan rule for Sprint 3: ONE file per sprint named `SPRINT-3 UX{n}`, with one page per week. Week 11 is page `W11`. Do not make three Figma files per sprint.

### Group 1 - Audit

#### Task - Dark pattern audit (ID `E1.1`)

##### 1. What We Are Doing
Audit three real products for dark patterns in sign-up, billing and cancellation. Name the pattern and describe the exact mechanism.

##### 2. Why We Are Doing It
A label like "it is manipulative" is easy to say and easy to ignore. A mechanism shows how the trick works and how to detect the same trick elsewhere.

##### 3. What to Learn First
Use the Day 1 sources: deceptive.design taxonomy, CMA practice types, FTC enforcement examples. Learn the pattern names before you audit.

Public pages only. Do not log in, do not buy, do not cancel, do not submit a form, and do not open a personal profile screen. If you cannot see a flow without an account, use the anonymised screenshots supplied in Discord instead.

##### 4. Where to Find Resources
Use the Day 1 rows in "Your week in order". Use publicly accessible flows, or the anonymised screenshots supplied in Discord. Do not use your own account data.

##### 5. Step-by-Step Guide
1. Take three products with publicly accessible flows, or three sets of anonymised screenshots supplied in Discord. Do not sign in to get them.
2. Write the public-pages rule at the top of your file: no logging in, no buying, no cancelling, no form submission, no personal profile screens.
3. Look at every dark pattern you can see in the sign-up, the billing and the cancellation screens.
4. For each finding, name the pattern from the taxonomy.
5. Describe the exact mechanism. Say what the design uses in the user's attention, memory or emotion.
6. State what it takes from the user. Quantify it where possible.
7. State what the business gains. This is why the pattern exists.
8. Make the mechanism the important column. Use the worked example as a model.

> **Worked example**
> **Product:** a streaming service cancellation flow, seen on public help pages.
> **Pattern:** obstruction, specifically roach motel. Sign-up is two clicks. Cancellation is seven screens.
> **Mechanism:** each screen shows one "are you sure" variant with different framing: price, content, family members, a retention offer. None is outrageous alone. The cumulative effect uses decision fatigue. Each screen has a small chance the user gives up. Seven small chances add up to a large retention number.
> **What it takes from the user:** about 4 minutes. For some users, a subscription they meant to end. If 8% abandon the cancellation, 8% pay for something they decided to stop paying for.
> **What the business gains:** measurable retention. That is why it survives review. Someone has a dashboard showing this flow "working".

If you get stuck: reread the taxonomy names, search "confirmshaming cancellation flow", and ask in Discord with your screenshots and what you tried.
How to think about it: a mechanism is stronger than a label. It tells you what to look for next time.

### Group 2 - Test

#### Task - Persuasion versus manipulation (ID `E1.2`)

##### 1. What We Are Doing
Write your own test to separate persuasion from manipulation. Apply it to ten real examples and study the hard cases.

##### 2. Why We Are Doing It
You need a test you can use in a meeting, on a specific screen, in ten seconds. The test helps you make a clear argument without relying on personal taste.

##### 3. What to Learn First
Use Day 2 sources: Ruined by Design Part 1, Fogg Behaviour Model, NNGroup article. A good test checks whether the design still works when you explain it.

##### 4. Where to Find Resources
Use the Day 2 rows in "Your week in order". Keep your E1.1 audit nearby.

##### 5. Step-by-Step Guide
1. Write your own test for distinguishing persuasion from manipulation. It must be usable on a specific screen, by you, in a meeting, in ten seconds.
2. Apply it to ten real examples: five you consider acceptable persuasion and five you consider manipulation.
3. Find the two hardest cases where your test gives an unclear answer.
4. Say what makes those cases hard.
5. A test that classifies all ten cleanly is probably too crude. The boundary cases are where the thinking is.

> **Worked example, write your own answer. These are prompts, not answers.**
> **My test, in three questions:**
> 1. ...
> 2. ...
> 3. ...
>
> **When my test says "manipulation":** ...
>
> **Hard case:** ...
> **Why my test is unclear here:** ...
> **How I resolve it, or why I cannot:** ...
>
> Note what is missing from this box: everything. The test, the classification rule and the resolved hard case are yours to write.

If you get stuck: reread Fogg and the NNGroup article, search "persuasion versus manipulation test", and ask in Discord with your two hard cases.
How to think about it: a test that classifies all ten cleanly is probably too crude. The hard cases are the useful part.

### Group 3 - Build

#### Task - Build the dark pattern (ID `E1.3`)

##### 1. What We Are Doing
Design a cancellation flow whose explicit goal is to maximise retention. Use five dark patterns. Build it properly, at high fidelity, reusing elements you already built in Sprint 3. You are not expected to make a new design system this week.

##### 2. Why We Are Doing It

- A component is a reusable element that you build once and use in many places.
- To make one in Figma, select an element and use Create component. Penpot calls it a component as well.
- Copy elements from your earlier Sprint 3 screens into page `W11` before you build anything new.

##### 4. Where to Find Resources

### Group 4 - Account and repair

#### Task - Cost accounting (ID `E1.4`)

##### 1. What We Are Doing
For each of the five patterns you built, write who it harms, what it takes, the estimated effect, the regulatory exposure and what you would say to a manager.

##### 2. Why We Are Doing It
This is the document that turns a dark pattern into evidence. It shows the human cost and the business risk in the same place.

##### 3. What to Learn First
Use Day 4 sources: Ruined by Design Part 2, and your own E1-3 with your E1.2 test in hand. Point 5 is the real workplace skill. Retention lift means the estimated increase in the number of customers who stay.

##### 4. Where to Find Resources
Use the Day 4 rows in "Your week in order". Use the CMA and FTC practice types from Day 1. Use your E1.3 file and E1.2 test.

##### 5. Step-by-Step Guide
1. For each of the five patterns you built, write who it harms. Be specific about which users. Some patterns harm the confused more. Some harm the hurried. Some harm people with cognitive disabilities much more than others.
2. Write what it takes. Quantify it. Money, time or data.
3. Write your estimated effect. Give your honest guess at the retention lift, and say what the guess is based on.
4. Write the regulatory exposure. Say which CMA or FTC practice type it maps to.
5. Write what you would say if a manager asked you to ship it. Write the actual sentences, not a summary of your position.

> **Worked example, point 5. Write your own answer. These are prompts.**
> **The specific exposure I would name:** ...
> **The alternative I would offer:** ...
> **The commercial cost I would concede, and my estimate of it:** ...
> **Who I would ask to own the decision:** ...
>
> Fill in your own sentences. Notice what a strong answer does. It does not moralise. It names the specific exposure. It offers an alternative. It concedes the commercial cost. It asks who owns the risk instead of absorbing it silently.

If you get stuck: reread the CMA and FTC practice types, search "dark pattern regulatory exposure", and ask in Discord with your mapping table.
How to think about it: point 5 is not a moral speech. Name the exposure, offer an alternative, concede the cost and ask who owns the risk.

#### Task - The honest version (ID `E1.5`)

##### 1. What We Are Doing
Redesign the same cancellation flow to be honest. Estimate the business cost. Argue for the honest version in business terms. Design the experiment that would test your claim.

##### 2. Why We Are Doing It
An ethical position without a business case often loses the meeting. You need to show the commercial reasons clearly and honestly.

##### 3. What to Learn First
Use Day 4 sources. Read `01-project-brief.md` section 6 for traffic. Learn these terms in plain words:
- Baseline rate: the share of users who cancel today, before any change.
- Effect size: the change you expect, in percentage points.
- Sample size: how many users you need in each group before the result is believable.
- Eligible traffic: how many users reach the flow each week.
- Observation time: how many weeks you must wait to collect that sample.
- Minimum detectable effect: the smallest change your test can see.

Before step 5, fill the worksheet below. Use simple numbers from the brief or from your own estimate, and say where each number came from.

| Row | What to write | Your answer |
|---|---|---|
| Baseline rate | The share of users who cancel today, for example 4 in 100 | ... |
| Effect size | The change you expect, in percentage points | ... |
| Sample size | Number of users needed in each group (search "sample size calculator for A/B test" and use a free one) | ... |
| Eligible traffic | Users who reach the flow each week, from the brief | ... |
| Observation time | Sample size divided by eligible traffic, in weeks | ... |

If observation time is many months, the test cannot run in a sensible time at this traffic. That is a real answer, not a failure.

##### 4. Where to Find Resources
Use the Day 4 rows. Use your E1.4 cost accounting. Use `01-project-brief.md` section 6 for traffic numbers. Use the worksheet in section 3 above.

##### 5. Step-by-Step Guide
1. Redesign the same cancellation flow to be honest: fastest reasonable path out, clear consequences, one retention offer presented once, nothing pre-checked.
2. Estimate what it costs the business relative to your dark version.
3. Label every number in that estimate as an assumption. Say what each assumption is based on.
4. Say what evidence would test each assumption, for example a small test, a support ticket count or a chargeback report.
5. Fill the worksheet in section 3: baseline rate, effect size, sample size, eligible traffic, observation time.
6. State the argument for the honest version in business terms. Not ethical terms.
7. Design the experiment that would tell the business whether the honest version really costs what you claim. State the primary metric, the guardrail (chargebacks and 90-day reactivation are good ones), the minimum detectable effect, and the sample size and run time from your worksheet.
8. If the traffic cannot detect the effect in a sensible time, say so and say what you would do instead.
9. Point 6 is the real deliverable. "It is the right thing to do" loses the meeting. Reduced support contacts, reduced chargebacks, reduced regulatory exposure, reduced reputational risk and higher reactivation rates from people who left on good terms can all win it, but each claim needs your own reasoning and your own evidence.

> **Worked example, the business argument. Write your own answer. These are prompts.**
> **The cost I estimate, and the basis for that estimate:** ...
> **Commercial reason 1, with the evidence that would support it:** ...
> **Commercial reason 2, with the evidence that would support it:** ...
> **Commercial reason 3, with the evidence that would support it:** ...
> **Commercial reason 4, with the evidence that would support it:** ...
> **What I am not claiming, because I have no evidence yet:** ...
>
> Do not copy a number from this page. Every figure here is yours, and every figure must be labelled as an assumption with a stated basis.

If you get stuck: reread `01-project-brief.md` section 6, search "minimum detectable effect", and ask in Discord with your metric and traffic numbers.
How to think about it: the business argument is the real deliverable. The ethical argument comes later, in week 12.

### Group 5 - Failure injection

The failure injection arrives this week. It is a realistic business demand added by the team leader to test your principles. In this sprint the injection is a business demand. It will conflict with something in your P9.1 or P10.1 principles. That is the design of it.

#### Task - Post mortem (ID `X3`)

##### 1. What We Are Doing
Write a post mortem of the business demand. A post mortem is a review of what happened and what to change. State what was demanded, which principle it conflicted with, what you did, what it cost and what would have made it easier.

##### 2. Why We Are Doing It
This is how you learn to handle principle conflicts in real work. The earlier you can name the conflict, the more options you have.

##### 3. What to Learn First
Use your P9.1 or P10.1 principles. Read the demand factually before you judge it. A post mortem is a review of what happened and what to change, written after the event.

##### 4. Where to Find Resources
Use the failure injection message. Use your own principles from P9.1 or P10.1.

##### 5. Step-by-Step Guide
1. Write what was demanded, factually.
2. Name which of your principles it conflicts with, by number.
3. Write what you did. Complied, refused, negotiated, or found a third option.
4. Write what it cost either way.
5. Write what would have made this easier to handle. Usually it is having stated the principle earlier, in writing, where someone else could see it.
6. Write your Day 5 notes in `students/UX{n}/week11/session5-learning.md`.

If you get stuck: reread your P9.1 and P10.1 principles, search "design principle conflict", and ask in Discord with the demand written factually.
How to think about it: a junior designer who says "I complied and here is what I would have needed to refuse" is more useful than one who claims they would have walked out.

#### Task - Principle conflict (ID `P11.1`)

##### 1. What We Are Doing
Take the principle that was challenged. Decide if it holds, bends or breaks. Write the exact conditions if it bends. Say what you would have needed if it broke.

##### 2. Why We Are Doing It
A principle with unstated exceptions is not a principle. This task makes your line clear and usable.

##### 3. What to Learn First
Use your X3 post mortem and the principle that was challenged. Be honest about what you actually did.

##### 4. Where to Find Resources
Use your X3 notes. Use your P9.1 or P10.1 principles.

##### 5. Step-by-Step Guide
1. Take the principle that was challenged.
2. Decide: does it hold, bend, or break?
3. If it bends, state the exact conditions under which it bends.
4. If it broke, say so honestly.
5. Then say what you would need, such as seniority, a written policy or another job offer, for it to have held.

If you get stuck: reread your X3 notes, search "design principle exception", and ask in Discord with your holds, bends or breaks decision.
How to think about it: point 4 is not a failure. A clear statement of what you would have needed is useful evidence.
