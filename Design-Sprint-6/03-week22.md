## 1. What We Are Doing
This week you make your portfolio public. You decide the pages, write the home page, build and publish the site, then get it reviewed by someone outside the program. You also write a one-page resume, update LinkedIn, and check accessibility and speed.

- Group 1 - Portfolio structure and build: B2.1, B2.2, B2.3
- Group 2 - Resume, LinkedIn, accessibility, review, fix: B2.4, B2.5, B2.6, B2.7
- Group 3 - Code corner: Z22.1
- Group 4 - Cohort day: F22.1, F22.2, F22.3

## 2. Why We Are Doing It
In India, a product designer often gets a first look through a link. A recruiter or hiring manager opens your portfolio on a phone between meetings. They decide in under two minutes. This week you make that link real and clear.

This matters for the project because you turn six sprints of work into a public story. It matters for your portfolio because the site itself shows your craft, your accessibility standards, and your performance choices.

## 3. What to Learn First
By the end of the week you can:
- Publish a live portfolio that works on a phone and passes its own accessibility standard.
- Write about yourself without overclaiming or apologising.
- Take external criticism of your own presentation without arguing.
- Explain why your portfolio's own design is the way it is.

Core ideas to review first:
- Portfolio pages: home, three case studies, about, contact.
- The ten-second job of the home page.
- Platform advantages and disadvantages: fast, phone-friendly, accessible. Choose what you can finish this week.
- One-page resume: action, method, outcome.
- LinkedIn in the first person, not the third person.
- Accessibility and performance checks: axe, Lighthouse, keyboard, screen reader, alt text.
- External review: take notes, do not argue.
- Figma file organisation: one file per sprint, one page per week. Do not make three files per sprint.

### Group 1 - Portfolio structure and build

#### Task - Structure (ID `B2.1`)
##### 1. What We Are Doing
Decide the pages for your portfolio and what the home page must do in ten seconds.

##### 2. Why We Are Doing It
A small clear portfolio helps a stranger understand you fast. Extra weak pages lower the average, and the average is what gets judged.

##### 3. What to Learn First
- Page list: home, three case studies, about, contact.
- The ten-second job of the home page.
- Navigation from any entry point.
- What you leave out.

##### 4. Where to Find Resources
- Your ten portfolio examples from Day 1.
- Your B1.2 hook sentences.
- Your three case studies.

##### 5. Step-by-Step Guide
1. Decide the pages. Home, three case studies, about, and a contact route. That is usually all.
2. Decide what the home page must do in ten seconds: who you are, what kind of work you do, and one reason to keep reading.
3. Decide the navigation. Someone landing on a case study from a link must be able to get to the rest.
4. Decide what you are not including. No Dribbble shots, no unfinished concepts, no "passion projects" with no problem behind them. Every extra weak item lowers the average, and the average is what gets judged.

How to think about it: If a page does not help a hiring manager decide to contact you, cut it.

If you get stuck: read the ten portfolio examples again. Search "UX portfolio structure". Ask in Discord with your page list and what you tried.

#### Task - Home page (ID `B2.2`)
##### 1. What We Are Doing
Write and design the home page so a stranger understands who you are and what you do.

##### 2. Why We Are Doing It
The home page is the first test. If it is slow or vague, the reader leaves before your case studies.

##### 3. What to Learn First
- Visible before scrolling: name, discipline, three projects.
- No welcome message, no entry animation.
- Each project shows its hook, not "Project 1".
- One line about the work you want.

##### 4. Where to Find Resources
- Your B2.1 structure.
- Your B1.2 hook sentences.
- Your ten portfolio examples.

##### 5. Step-by-Step Guide
1. Visible before scrolling: your name, what you do, and the three projects. Nothing else.
2. No "welcome to my portfolio". No slow reveal animation. No changing the normal scrolling behaviour. No cursor effects. Every one of those costs you a reader.
3. Each project gets a card with the hook from B1.2, not a title like "Project 1".
4. Add one sentence on what kind of problem you want to work on. This is the line that makes a hiring manager think of a specific team.

Worked example:
Weak: "Hi, I'm P1. I'm a passionate UX designer who loves creating delightful user experiences."
Better: "P1 - product designer, Bangalore. I work on services where the users have no choice about using them: public health, benefits, and gig work. Three case studies below, each with the research behind it."

The second one is specific. It is not about how much you love design. It tells a hiring manager in the public-sector space that you are the candidate for them.

If you get stuck: read your home page on a phone. Ask one person to say what you do after ten seconds. Ask in Discord with a screenshot.

### Group 2 - Resume, LinkedIn, accessibility, review, fix

#### Task - Resume (ID `B2.4`)
##### 1. What We Are Doing
Write a one-page design resume. Every bullet shows action, method, and outcome. The resume is an anonymised course PDF, so it can be committed safely.

##### 2. Why We Are Doing It
Many applications go through parsers and quick scans. A clear one-page resume gets read. A two-column layout with icons often does not. And a resume file that carries your real name and phone number must never go into this public repo.

##### 3. What to Learn First
- One page only.
- Action, method, outcome.
- No "responsible for".
- Plain, machine-readable layout.
- Describe the program accurately: currently completing the 27-week DOTSET Product Design course.
- Anonymised PDF: your learner code `UX{n}` instead of a real name, no photo, no phone number, no personal email, no ID.

##### 4. Where to Find Resources
- Your resume and LinkedIn conventions from Day 3.
- Three resumes from people at the level you want.
- Your project outcomes from earlier sprints.
- The personal data rule in `../10-GitHub-For-Designers.md`.

##### 5. Step-by-Step Guide
1. Keep it to one page. One.
2. Write what you did, with what result. Not what you were responsible for.
3. Every bullet: action, method, outcome. If there is no outcome, there is no bullet.
4. Put the program on it as what it is: "Currently completing the 27-week DOTSET Product Design course." Do not dress it up as employment, and do not undersell it either.
5. Keep it plain and machine-readable. Many applications go through parsers that cannot read a two-column layout with icons.
6. Make the anonymised course copy. Use your learner code `UX{n}` in place of your real name. Remove your photo, your phone number, your personal email, and any ID number.
7. Save it as `B2-4-resume.pdf` and commit that copy only. Keep your personal version out of the repo.

Worked example:
Weak: "Responsible for redesigning the appointment booking user experience."
Better: "Redesigned a hospital appointment booking flow after contextual research with 5 patients and 2 receptionists; usability testing showed wrong-day arrivals fall from 4/5 to 0/5 participants."

If you get stuck: copy the Better sentence pattern. Ask in Discord with your draft and the job description you are targeting. If you are unsure what counts as personal data, ask before you commit.

#### Task - Accessibility and performance (ID `B2.6`)
##### 1. What We Are Doing
Audit your live portfolio for accessibility and speed. Fix what you find. Publish an accessibility statement.

##### 2. Why We Are Doing It
You have spent months saying accessibility matters. An inaccessible portfolio disproves your own case studies. Any competent interviewer will check.

##### 3. What to Learn First
- axe or Lighthouse audit.
- Keyboard navigation and visible focus.
- Screen reader reading order, alt text, heading hierarchy.
- Empty alt on decorative images.
- Speed test with fixed conditions: the home page, in Chrome, in the Network panel, with the throttling profile name recorded. Target under 3 seconds.
- An accessibility statement that includes known issues.

##### 4. Where to Find Resources
- axe DevTools or Lighthouse.
- Screen reader: use VoiceOver on Mac (`Cmd+F5`) or NVDA on Windows (https://www.nvaccess.org/). Review the accessibility tools setup from Z0.8.
- Your earlier accessibility statements from past sprints.

##### 5. Step-by-Step Guide
1. Run axe or Lighthouse. Fix everything.
2. Keyboard-navigate the whole site. Make focus visible everywhere.
3. Test with a screen reader. On Mac use VoiceOver (`Cmd+F5`). On Windows use NVDA. Check reading order, alt text, and heading hierarchy.
4. Give every image real alt text. Give decorative images empty alt.
5. Test speed with fixed conditions. Open Chrome, open DevTools, open the Network panel, and select a throttling profile. Test your home page. Write down the profile name and the load time.
6. Get the home page under 3 seconds in those conditions.
7. Write the accessibility statement for your own site, in the same format you have used for four sprints. Include anything that still fails.

If you get stuck: fix the first audit error, then run the audit again. Search the exact error text. Ask in Discord with a screenshot and what you tried.

#### Task - Fix it (ID `B2.7`)
##### 1. What We Are Doing
Turn the external review into decisions. Fix, defer, or reject each piece of feedback. Then re-test the ninety-second comprehension with a new person.

##### 2. Why We Are Doing It
Feedback is only useful when it changes the work. Re-testing shows if the fix worked for a new reader.

##### 3. What to Learn First
- Every feedback item gets a decision.
- Rejections need a written reason you would say out loud.
- Fix the full fix list.
- Re-test with a different person.

##### 4. Where to Find Resources
- Your notes from the external review.
- Your live portfolio.
- A second person for the re-test. If no external reviewer was available, use the learner outside your project who did the first pass, and a different person for the re-test.

##### 5. Step-by-Step Guide
1. Give every piece of feedback a decision: fix, defer, or reject.
2. For rejections, write the reason. Rejecting feedback is legitimate, but only with a reason you would say out loud to the reviewer.
3. Fix everything in the fix list.
4. Re-test the ninety-second comprehension with a different person afterwards.

How to think about it: A reviewer says what they saw, not what you meant. Treat that as data.

If you get stuck: start with the feedback that appears twice. Ask in Discord with your decision list and what you tried.

##### 6. What to Commit
- [ ] File: `students/UX{n}/week22/B2-7-revisions.md`
- [ ] Every piece of feedback has a decision
- [ ] Rejections justified in writing
- [ ] Fix list complete
- [ ] Re-tested with someone new, and they can state the problem and outcome of each project
- [ ] Commit message: `B2.7: fix external review feedback`

### Group 3 - Code corner

#### Task - Code corner: publish a simple portfolio page on GitHub Pages (ID `Z22.1`)
##### 1. What We Are Doing
Publish a simple one-page portfolio on GitHub Pages in 40 minutes. Use the HTML and CSS you already know.

##### 2. Why We Are Doing It
A live URL is proof. GitHub Pages is free and works with your repo. This also shows you can publish a small page without a big platform.

##### 3. What to Learn First
- One `index.html` file and one `style.css` file.
- A GitHub repository can serve a page from a branch.
- The GitHub Pages quickstart steps.
- Public page rule: your learner code `UX{n}`, not your real name. No faces, no phone numbers, no IDs.

##### 4. Where to Find Resources
- GitHub Pages quickstart: https://docs.github.com/en/pages/quickstart
- Your earlier HTML and CSS work from week0, week3, and week12b.
- VS Code and Git setup: `../04-Tools-Setup.md` (VS Code and Git section).

##### 5. Step-by-Step Guide
1. Open VS Code.
2. Make a folder at `students/UX{n}/week22/portfolio-page/`.
3. Add `index.html` inside it.
4. Add your learner code `UX{n}`, one line on what you do, and text links to three anonymised case studies. Use project codes and problems, not client names or real people. Text links are fine.
5. Add `style.css` inside the same folder, with simple readable type and spacing. Do not add animation.
6. Link the CSS from the HTML.
7. Open `index.html` in a browser and check it in a narrow window.
8. Create a GitHub repository for the page.
9. Push `index.html` and `style.css` to the repository.
10. Open the GitHub Pages quickstart: https://docs.github.com/en/pages/quickstart
11. Follow the steps to publish from your repository.
12. Wait for the live URL, then open it on your phone.
13. Write a short note with the live URL and one screenshot. Check the screenshot first: no real names, no faces, no phone numbers, no IDs.

How to think about it: This page is not the full portfolio. It is proof that you can publish. Keep it small.

If you get stuck: read the quickstart page again. Check that `index.html` is in the root. Ask in Discord with a screenshot and the error text.
