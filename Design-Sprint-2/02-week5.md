## 2. Why We Are Doing It

In India, public service forms decide access to scholarships and benefits. A working product designer cannot fix drop-off by guessing. You need a small research plan that can change your mind.

This week matters for the project because weeks 6 to 8 depend on real participants and a clear plan. It matters for your portfolio because a research plan, consent notes, and a stakeholder map show how you think before you design.

## 3. What to Learn First

By the end of the week you can:

- List your assumptions and rank them by what it would cost to be wrong
- Choose a research method because it answers your question, not because it is familiar
- Write a research plan that states in advance what result would prove you wrong
- Recruit five real participants

Core ideas to review first:

- An assumption is a claim you have not tested yet.
- Risk = uncertainty × cost of being wrong.
- Qualitative research tells you why and how, in small numbers. Quantitative research tells you how many and how often, in large numbers.
- Falsification means the result that would prove your expectation wrong.
- Consent is a clear promise, not a form to collect.
- Open questions get stories. Yes/no questions get short answers.
- Stakeholder conflicts are real design constraints.

### Group 1 - Assumptions and risk

#### Task - Assumption list (ID `R3.1`)

##### 1. What We Are Doing
Read the brief and write everything it treats as true without evidence. Include the mission's central claim: drop-off is caused by form length. Include your own assumptions too.

##### 2. Why We Are Doing It
Research starts with what you think you know. Naming assumptions lets you test them instead of protecting them.

##### 3. What to Learn First
A stated assumption is in the brief. An implied assumption is not said, but the brief depends on it. Your own assumption comes from your habits or guesses.

##### 4. Where to Find Resources
`01-project-brief.md`, Just Enough Research ch. 1 to 3, NNGroup "When to Use Which User-Experience Research Method".

Worked example for a different brief, about booking a sports court:

| Assumption | Source | Why it might be false |
|---|---|---|
| Most people book a court on a phone | Stated | The service is used on phones, but the booking may happen on a laptop at home, or one person may book for the whole group. |
| People know their team size before they start booking | Implied | The brief never says this. If they decide late, they may have to go back and change the booking. |
| People book at least a week ahead | Mine | I assumed this from how I book. Never checked. |

##### 5. Step-by-Step Guide
1. Read the brief.
2. Write down each claim it treats as true without evidence.
3. Add the mission's central claim: drop-off is caused by form length.
4. Add your own assumptions from Sprint 1 habits.
5. Aim for 15 or more. Fewer than 10 means you are still reading the brief as fact.
6. Mark each as stated in the brief, implied by the brief, or yours.
7. Add one reason each assumption might be false.

How to think about it: an assumption is a sentence you cannot prove yet. If you cannot say what evidence would change it, rewrite it.

If you get stuck: read the brief again and underline claims, search "assumption mapping UX research", or ask in Discord with a screenshot and what you tried.

#### Task - Rank by risk (ID `R3.2`)

##### 1. What We Are Doing
Score each assumption for uncertainty and cost of being wrong. Multiply the scores. Sort the list. The top three are what you research.

##### 2. Why We Are Doing It
Research capacity is small. Five interviews cannot answer 15 questions. Ranking is how you decide which three questions the five interviews are for.

##### 3. What to Learn First
Uncertainty means how sure you are it is true. Cost of being wrong means what happens if you build on it and it is false. A falsification condition is what you would need to see to change your mind.

##### 4. Where to Find Resources
Your R3.1, Just Enough Research ch. 1 to 3, NNGroup method-choice article.

##### 5. Step-by-Step Guide
1. For each assumption, score uncertainty from 1 to 5.
2. For each assumption, score cost of being wrong from 1 to 5.
3. Multiply uncertainty by cost to get risk.
4. Sort by risk.
5. Identify the top three. Everything else waits.
6. For each top three, write what you would need to see to change your mind.

How to think about it: the top three are not the most interesting. They are the most risky.

If you get stuck: re-read R3.1, search "risk matrix UX research", or ask in Discord with your sorted list and what you tried.

- [ ] Sorted by risk
- [ ] Top three identified
- [ ] For each of the top three, a stated falsification condition
- [ ] Commit message: `R3.2 rank assumptions by risk`

### Group 2 - Research plan

#### Task - The research plan (ID `R3.3`)

##### 1. What We Are Doing
Write a research plan with eight sections. None are optional. The plan says what you will ask, who you will ask, how many, and what would prove you wrong.

##### 2. Why We Are Doing It
A plan makes method choice and what would prove you wrong visible before you spend time. It stops you from doing research that can only confirm what you already think.

##### 3. What to Learn First
Qualitative tells you why and how, in small numbers. Quantitative tells you how many and how often, in large numbers. Choosing the wrong one is expensive because you find out at the end.

##### 4. Where to Find Resources
Your R3.2, Just Enough Research ch. 4 and 5, NNGroup "Quantitative vs. Qualitative Research" and the method-choice article.

##### 5. Step-by-Step Guide
1. Write the questions. Use your top three from R3.2, phrased as questions, not topics.
2. Write the method for each question with a reason. Interview, usability test, analytics, survey, or diary study.
3. Write who you need. Be specific: "parents of students in classes 9 to 12 who started an application in the last 6 months and did not finish". Not "users".
4. Write how many and why that number. Five is defensible if you state what five can and cannot support.
5. Write your session steps. This is exactly what you will do in each session, in order.
6. Write what would show each assumption is wrong. State, in advance, the result that would make you doubt your expectation.
7. Write what this method cannot tell you. Name something real it will not answer.
8. Write a timeline. Recruiting takes longer than running.

Worked example for section 6, for a different question, about booking a sports court:

> **Question:** Do people stop booking because of the number of steps, or because of one specific step?
> **What would show "number of steps" is wrong:** If participants describe confusion at one or two specific steps, and never mention the number of steps, the number of steps is a weaker explanation. I will know this from what participants say, and from where in the booking they hesitate.
> **What would show "one specific step" is wrong:** If participants describe tiredness with the number of steps, or say they stopped because there were too many screens, the number of steps is a stronger explanation. I will know this from what participants say, and from where in the booking they hesitate.

Both directions are stated. Each result would weaken the assumption but would not establish the cause. A plan that can only confirm what you expect is not a plan.

How to think about it: if no result can surprise you, it is not research.

If you get stuck: re-read your top three, search "research plan falsification", or ask in Discord with your draft section 6 and what you tried.

##### 6. What to Commit
- [ ] `students/UX{n}/week5/R3-3-research-plan.md`

- [ ] Every method choice has a reason tied to its question
- [ ] The participant description is specific enough to recruit from

- [ ] Section 6 does not claim that a result proves the cause

- [ ] Commit message: `R3.3 write research plan`

### Group 3 - Consent and recruitment

#### Task - Consent form and script (ID `R3.4`)

##### 1. What We Are Doing
Write an adult consent script, a minor version with guardian consent, and an anonymisation rule. State where data is stored and how long you keep it.

##### 2. Why We Are Doing It
This sprint involves students. Some participants may be under 18. Consent from a minor is not sufficient. Recording a minor has different rules. "It is only for a design project" is not an exemption.

##### 3. What to Learn First
The adult script must state: who you are, what you are doing, what you will record, where it will be stored, how long you keep it, who sees it, that they can stop at any time with no consequence, and that you are testing the design and not them.

##### 4. Where to Find Resources

##### 5. Step-by-Step Guide

3. Add the guardian consent the minor version requires.
4. Write your anonymisation rule: what you strip before anything is committed to git.
5. State the storage location and retention period.
6. Check that nothing in the script requires the participant to understand design jargon.

> Nothing identifiable goes in the repository. No names, no phone numbers, no school names, no faces. Participants are P1 to P5.

How to think about it: consent is a clear promise about data, not a form to collect.

#### Task - Recruit (ID `R3.5`)

##### 1. What We Are Doing
Write a screener. Contact real people. Confirm five participants with two backups. Log every attempt, including refusals, using anonymous codes only. Schedule sessions in week 6.

##### 2. Why We Are Doing It
Weeks 6 to 8 depend on real participants. Who you could not reach is also a finding about who the service excludes.

##### 3. What to Learn First
A screener is three or four questions that confirm someone matches your R3.3 participant description. A recruitment log records every attempt. An anonymous code is a short label like P1 or P2 that stands for a person, so no real name ever reaches the file.

##### 4. Where to Find Resources
Your R3.3 participant description, your R3.4 consent script.

##### 5. Step-by-Step Guide
1. Write three or four screener questions that confirm someone matches your R3.3 description.
2. List the channels you will use to reach people. Use real people.
3. Give every person you plan to contact an anonymous code: P1 to P5 for participants, B1 and B2 for backups, N1 and N2 for people who say no.
4. Contact people and aim to confirm five with two backups.
5. Log every attempt in `R3-5-recruitment-log.md`, using the code only, including refusals.
6. Keep names, phone numbers, email addresses, and identifiable scheduling details outside Git and outside Discord. Save them in your own private notes, or do not save them at all.
7. Schedule confirmed sessions for week 6, and write only the session time window in the log.
8. If recruiting fails, write who you could not reach and why, still using codes.
9. Adjust your method if needed: a shorter session, a phone call instead of a video call, or a different time of day.
10. Document the change and the reason.

If recruiting fails, that is not a disaster and it is not an excuse. It is a finding.

> If recruitment is incomplete, commit the anonymised attempt log, your revised plan, and your admin escalation. Arrange the next step before you start any participant session.

How to think about it: a refusal tells you about access. Record it.

If you get stuck: re-read your screener, search "user research screener questions", or ask in Discord with your log and what you tried.

### Group 4 - Guide, stakeholders, critique

#### Task - Discussion guide (ID `R3.6`)

##### 1. What We Are Doing
Write a 45-minute discussion guide with four sections: warm-up, the participant's own story, the specific experience, and wrap-up.

##### 2. Why We Are Doing It
Open questions get real stories. Leading questions hand the participant your hypothesis.

##### 3. What to Learn First
An open question cannot be answered with yes or no. A hypothetical asks people to predict behaviour. A probe is a follow-up question.

##### 4. Where to Find Resources
Just Enough Research ch. 6, Interviewing Users by Portigal ch. 1, your R3.3.

##### 5. Step-by-Step Guide
1. Write four sections in order: warm-up, their story, the specific experience, wrap-up.
2. Start broad: "Tell me about the last time you applied for something for your child's education".
3. Rewrite every question so it is open.
4. Write a ban list at the top of your own guide: "would you", "do you like", "how easy was", and the name of a feature.
5. Write your follow-up probes: "tell me more about that", "what happened next", "what were you expecting", "walk me through that".
6. Write "wait 5 seconds" into the guide at three places.
7. Check that it fits in 45 minutes with room to go off script.

Worked example:

| Bad | Why | Better |
|---|---|---|
| "Would you use an app for this?" | Hypothetical. People are bad at predicting their own behaviour. | "How did you fill it in last time? Walk me through what you did." |
| "Was the form too long?" | Leading, and it hands them your hypothesis. | "Tell me about the last time you filled this in. Where did you stop?" |
| "Do you find uploading documents difficult?" | Yes/no, and it suggests the answer. | "Tell me about the documents you needed. How did you get them?" |

How to think about it: a good guide makes the participant talk more than you.

If you get stuck: read your guide aloud, search "discussion guide open questions", or ask in Discord with your draft and what you tried.
