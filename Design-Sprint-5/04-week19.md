## 1. What We Are Doing

This week you test your capstone with real users. You write the test plan before you test, run the sessions, and change your design because of what you see. You also absorb a severe late constraint and review your own design for ethical problems.

The week in order:
- Day 1: K3.1. Learn the test method and write the test plan.
- Day 2: K3.2. Run five or more sessions.
- Day 3: K3.3, K3.4. Write findings and an iteration plan.
- Day 4: K3.5, X5, critique, whiteboard why. Iterate and absorb the injection.
- Day 5: K3.6, evidence gate. Review ethics and prove the trace.
- Day 6: S23, F19. Contribute to the system and continue fundamentals.

## 2. Why We Are Doing It

On a real product team in India, you will not get unlimited time to test. You will get one short round, a late constraint, and a manager who likes the old design. This week trains the skill that makes you useful: change your design when evidence says it is wrong, and show exactly why.

For the capstone, this is the week where your portfolio stops being only screens and becomes a story of decisions. Hiring managers look for evidence, not perfect screens. The ethics review also protects you: it helps you see problems in your own work before a user or a reviewer sees them.

## 3. What to Learn First

By the end of the week you can:
- Test your own design with real users without defending it.
- Change a design because of evidence, and show the trace.
- Absorb a severe late constraint with one week left.
- Review your own work for ethical problems, and record what you find and what you did.

Core ideas to review first:
- A moderated task-based test is the default. You give a task, watch, and do not teach.
- A comprehension test checks if people understand words and concepts. A first-click test checks where people click first to find something.
- A falsification condition is the result that would prove your design wrong. Write it before you test.
- A denominator is the number the result is out of, for example 3 of 5 participants.
- Severity is how much a problem blocks or slows the user.
- A trace is a clear link from a finding to a specific design change.
- An evidence gate is a check that your design changed because of real evidence.
- The injection is a late constraint that breaks something you assumed was fixed.

#### Task - Test plan (ID `K3.1`)

##### 1. What We Are Doing
Write the test plan before you test. The plan has five parts.

##### 2. Why We Are Doing It
The falsification condition is the part that makes the test honest. If you cannot say in advance what failure looks like, you will interpret whatever happens as success.

##### 3. What to Learn First
- Scope means what you test and what you do not test.
- A scenario is a realistic task with no instruction inside it.
- A falsification condition is the result that would prove you wrong.
- Recruitment means who you ask and how.

##### 4. Where to Find Resources
Your test plan. Use the worked example below. No fixed chapter in this brief.

##### 5. Step-by-Step Guide
1. Write what you are testing.
2. Write what you are not testing.
3. Write each task as a realistic scenario. Do not put steps inside it.
4. For each key assumption, write what would falsify it. Do this before testing.
5. Write who you will test, how many, and how you will recruit them.
6. Write what you will do for each possible outcome.
7. Read the falsification condition aloud. If it is vague, rewrite it.

Worked example, the falsification condition:

> **My design assumes** that a user will understand "Reschedule" as the way to move an appointment rather than cancelling and re-booking.
> **What would falsify it:** two or more of five participants, when asked to move their appointment to a different day, tap "Cancel" first.
> **What I do if that happens:** the label is wrong, not the flow. I test "Change date" as an alternative, and if the concept itself is not landing I surface both actions on the appointment card with the consequences stated.

How to think about it: a good falsification condition is observable. It says what a person does, not what you feel. It has a number and a task. Five participants is a small sample, so use it to find problems, not to prove success.

If you get stuck: read the falsification condition again. Search "falsification condition usability test". Ask in Discord with your assumption and your falsification condition.

##### 6. What to Commit

- [ ] Done when:
  - [ ] Scope of the test stated
  - [ ] Tasks written as scenarios with no embedded instructions

  - [ ] A planned response for each outcome
- [ ] Commit message: `K3.1: add test plan`

### Group 2 - Day 2: Run the sessions

#### Task - Run the sessions (ID `K3.2`)

##### 1. What We Are Doing
Run five or more sessions with real users from your target group.

##### 2. Why We Are Doing It
Real users show real behaviour. Peers know too much and are too kind.

##### 3. What to Learn First
- Real users means people from your target group, not your cohort.
- Consent means they agree and know how notes will be used.
- Anonymised means no names or faces in your notes.
- No leading means do not suggest the answer.
- No rescuing means do not fix the screen mid-task.

##### 4. Where to Find Resources
Your test plan and prototype. 5 hours. No fixed chapter in this brief.

##### 5. Step-by-Step Guide
1. Recruit five or more real users.
2. Ask for consent.
3. Explain what you record.
4. Open the prototype or site.
5. Read the scenario. Do not add instructions.
6. Start the task. Say nothing unless the person stops.
7. When they get stuck, count to five before you intervene.
8. When they say "I probably just missed it", write down where it was.
9. Write notes straight away.
10. For each task, record completion, time, and errors.
11. Write direct quotes word for word.
12. After the session, check that you did not explain the design mid-task.

If you get stuck: read the rules again. Search "usability test consent anonymised notes". Ask in Discord with one stuck moment and what you did.

##### 6. What to Commit
- [ ] File: `students/UX{n}/week19/K3-2-sessions.md`
- [ ] Done when:
  - [ ] Five or more sessions, consented and anonymised
  - [ ] Notes straight away
  - [ ] Every task's completion recorded, with the time and the errors
  - [ ] Direct quotes captured word for word
  - [ ] Zero instances of you explaining the design mid-task
- [ ] Commit message: `K3.2: add session notes`

### Group 3 - Day 3: Findings and iteration plan

#### Task - Findings (ID `K3.3`)

##### 1. What We Are Doing
Turn session notes into findings with denominators and severity.

##### 2. Why We Are Doing It
A finding with no denominator is a story. A finding with no type is hard to fix. The type tells you what kind of fix to make.

##### 3. What to Learn First
- A denominator is the number the result is out of.
- Severity is how much the problem blocks or slows the user.
- Comprehension problem: the words or concept were not understood.
- Discoverability problem: the person did not find the action.
- Interaction problem: the action was hard to do.
- Content problem: the words or information were missing or wrong.

##### 4. Where to Find Resources
Your session notes. 2 hours. No fixed chapter in this brief.

##### 5. Step-by-Step Guide
1. List each problem you saw.
2. Add the denominator, for example 3 of 5 participants.
3. Add a severity: high, medium, or low.
4. Tag the type: comprehension, discoverability, interaction, or content.
5. Add one direct quote if you have it.
6. Give each finding a unique ID, such as `F19-01`, and use it in your iteration plan and decision records.
7. Sort by severity.

If you get stuck: read one session note again. Search "usability findings severity denominator". Ask in Discord with one finding and your type label.

##### 6. What to Commit
- [ ] File: `students/UX{n}/week19/K3-3-findings.md`
- [ ] Done when: every finding has a unique ID, a denominator and a severity, and every finding has a type.
- [ ] Commit message: `K3.3: add findings`

#### Task - Iteration plan (ID `K3.4`)

##### 1. What We Are Doing
Give every finding a decision: fix now, fix later, or accept.

##### 2. Why We Are Doing It
Not every finding can be fixed in one week. The plan protects the important ones from being quietly dropped.

##### 3. What to Learn First
- Fix now means you change it this week.
- Fix later means you record where it will not be lost.
- Accept means you decide not to change it, and you say why.
- A reversal condition is the evidence that would make you change your mind.

##### 4. Where to Find Resources
Your findings file. 2 hours. No fixed chapter in this brief.

##### 5. Step-by-Step Guide
1. Open the findings list.
2. Give every finding one decision.
3. For each "fix now", write the specific change.
4. For each "fix later", write where it is recorded.
5. For each "accept", write why and what evidence would change your mind.
6. Find the finding you least want to be true.
7. Write it down and say why it is important.

How to think about it: the finding you least want to be true is usually the one that threatens the story you told yourself. Naming it makes it harder to ignore. This is not a confession. It is a design skill.

If you get stuck: read the "accept" list again. Search "usability test findings prioritisation". Ask in Discord with the finding you least want to be true.

##### 6. What to Commit
- [ ] File: `students/UX{n}/week19/K3-4-iteration-plan.md`
- [ ] Done when:
  - [ ] Every finding has a decision
  - [ ] Fixes are specific changes, not intentions
  - [ ] Accepted findings have a reason and a reversal condition
  - [ ] The finding you least want to be true is named
- [ ] Commit message: `K3.4: add iteration plan`

##### 1. What We Are Doing

##### 2. Why We Are Doing It

##### 3. What to Learn First

##### 4. Where to Find Resources

##### 5. Step-by-Step Guide

7. Check that each change has a reason.

#### Task - Absorb the injection (ID `X5`)

##### 1. What We Are Doing
Write how you absorb the late constraint in the remaining time.

##### 2. Why We Are Doing It
Late constraints are normal in product work. The skill is to be honest about what breaks and to cut something to make room.

##### 3. What to Learn First
- The injection is the late constraint from your team.
- Invalidates means it makes part of your work wrong or useless.
- Cut means you remove work from the week, not add hours.

##### 4. Where to Find Resources
The injection brief from your team. Part of Day 4, 4 hours with K3.5. No fixed chapter in this brief.

##### 5. Step-by-Step Guide
1. Write what changed, in one factual paragraph.
2. List what it invalidates in your work.
3. Do not minimise. Write the worst part.
4. Write your plan for the remaining time.
5. Write what you cut to make room.
6. Write one specific week 17 action that would have made you better able to handle the change.
7. Read step 6. If it is vague, rewrite it. Use an action from your own week 17 work.

How to think about it: a late constraint is not a personal attack. It is information. The useful question is: what can I still protect in one week?

If you get stuck: read the injection again. Search "design constraint assumption fixed". Ask in Discord with what changed and what you cut.

### Group 5 - Day 5: Ethics review and evidence gate

#### Task - Ethics review (ID `K3.6`)

##### 1. What We Are Doing
Review your own design with your own Sprint 3 ethics position in hand.

##### 2. Why We Are Doing It
You are the designer. You can see problems in your own work. This review is the last chance to name them before a user or reviewer does.

##### 3. What to Learn First
- Persuasion means design that tries to change behaviour.
- Power asymmetry means one side has more control or information.
- Harm means a real negative effect for a specific user type.
- E1.2 is your Sprint 3 ethics test. Use it on your own screens.

##### 4. Where to Find Resources
Your Sprint 3 ethics position and your capstone screens. 3 hours. No fixed chapter in this brief.

##### 5. Step-by-Step Guide
1. Open your E1.2 test.
2. Apply it to your own screens.
3. Write where your design uses persuasion.
4. Apply your own E1.2 test to each of those places.
5. Write who could be harmed, and which users.
6. List what data you collect.
7. Justify each item on the list.
8. Write where the user has less power than the operator.
9. Write what you did about that.
10. Look for one thing in your own design you would refuse to ship if someone else asked for it.
11. Write down any problem you find. Do not soften it.
12. If you find no problem, write the checks you ran, the evidence, and the remaining risks.

How to think about it: there is often something. It may be a default, a nudge, a hidden cost, or a dark pattern. The exercise is to find it yourself. If you find nothing, that is a result too. Show the checks you ran.

If you get stuck: read your E1.2 test again. Search "design ethics persuasion dark pattern". Ask in Discord with one screen and your first answer.

##### 6. What to Commit
- [ ] File: `students/UX{n}/week19/K3-6-ethics-review.md`
- [ ] Done when:
  - [ ] Your own E1.2 test applied to your own screens
  - [ ] Harm assessed for specific user types
  - [ ] Data collection justified item by item
  - [ ] Power asymmetry named and addressed
  - [ ] Any ethical problem found is written down with your response, or the checks, the evidence and the remaining risks are recorded
- [ ] Commit message: `K3.6: add ethics review`

### Evidence gate - Day 5 afternoon

What is checked:
- Five or more real participants
- Findings with denominators and severity
- Traceable changes: a specific design change linked to a specific finding
- Accepted findings justified with reversal conditions
- The injection absorbed with a written plan
- The ethics review documents any ethical problems found, your response, and the checks you ran

The gate fails if decisions lack evidence. If no change is warranted, document the findings, the reasons, the remaining uncertainty, and the reversal conditions.
